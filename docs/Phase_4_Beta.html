Beta Release Objective

After listening and digesting having your project critiqued in front of the class, now you get to respond and revise.

Beta has two parts: a written rebuttal and a before/after of whatever you changed, or plan to change. Your response card from the presentation is your starting point for the rebuttal.

Part 1: Written Rebuttal

Respond to the major points from your peer critique. For each one, state whether you agree, partially agree, or disagree, and say why, using course theory. 300–400 words max. You can write in bullets or prose, no long essay needed (unless you want to).

What good rebuttal language looks like

Rebuttal Examples
✅ Good Examples ❌ Ungood Examples
“We disagree that a diverging colormap was needed here. Our data ranges from 0 to a maximum with no meaningful midpoint, so a sequential colormap is appropriate per Chapter 10.” “We disagree because we think our color choice worked well.”
“They were right that our bar chart should start at zero. Truncating the axis exaggerates differences between categories.” “Good point about the axis, we’ll fix it.”
“We partially agree. The rainbow colormap was flagged correctly since it creates false boundaries. But we used it to reveal fine-grained structure in a continuous field, which the course acknowledges as a valid use case.” “We used rainbow because it shows more detail.”
WarningFAQs
  • Do we have to respond to every critique point?
  • What if we disagree with most of the critique?

Part 2: Before/After Revision

Show what you changed with a few before/after examples, the critique point it addresses, and the principle behind the decision (or, if it was just a TODO, no need to annotate it).

Format can be a slide deck, annotated screenshots, high fidelity sketches, whatever shows the change clearly. High fidelity sketches are fine if a change is still in progress.

A few things:

  • If the critique identified a structural problem, you’ll want to go beyond a cosmetic fix. For example, swapping jet for viridis doesn’t fix a flawed encoding decision.
  • “No structural change needed” is a legitimate conclusion if you can justify it! If you looked at the critique and decided your underlying structure was sound, mention why and how.
  • There’s no minimum required number of changes, but heading into Final release, any major structural changes should be addressed
  • For changes, you’re not limited to what the peer group mentioned– anything you identify that needs re-working, or if you have some extra time to complete stretch goals, please feel free to do it!
WarningFAQs
  • What counts as a structural change?
  • Can we change something the peer group didn’t mention?

When in doubt, include it.

That's fine — disagreement is a legitimate position, and sometimes the reviewing group gets it wrong. The requirement is that your disagreement is argued, not just asserted. If you're pushing back on multiple points, make sure each pushback cites a specific principle. A rebuttal that disagrees with everything but can explain why using course theory is a strong rebuttal.

A structural change is one that affects a fundamental encoding or interaction decision — the choice of chart type, the visual channel used to encode a key attribute, the aggregation level of your data, or a core interaction mechanism. Changing a colormap from sequential to diverging because your data has a meaningful midpoint is structural. Making it a slightly different shade of blue is not. If you're unsure whether your change is structural, ask yourself: does this change affect what information the viewer can extract from the visualization, or just how it looks?
