Part 0: The 3 V’s: Verify Vis Veracity

There are a lot of bad charts on the web. In order to avoid basing your Project on bad info, please only use sources that are from:

  • A conference proceeding or journal paper from a peer-reviewed publication (e.g. do a Google Scholar search). Avoid sources from Predatory Publishers
  • A highly-regarded long-form* visualization blog, editorial, case-study, in-depth critique, podcast, or other online periodical. This can be nuanced, so ask the Professor when in doubt

Try to have a good balance between peer-reviewed research and media. Avoid only using compiled internet lists e.g. “Top 10 best/worst visualizations” or clickbait e.g. “You won’t BELIEVE why this vis was banned!!!!!!!!!!!”

Step 3+: Continue to search to avoid design fixation

Keep updating this collection of related work throughout your project, as features, scope, tasks, etc evolve. Prune as needed.

NoteWhat is design fixation and why should we care?

Design fixation is the tendency to get stuck on an initial concept and unconsciously constrain your thinking around it, even when better solutions exist. It’s the mental trap of anchoring too early on a specific idea, making it hard to explore genuinely different directions.

Why does it matter for this project and beyond?

  • A lot of times people settle on familiar vis idioms because they are familiar, not because they are the most effective for your user/task/data
  • It’s also easy to gravitate toward whatever the default output of a library looks like, instead of what your data and user actually need
  • Seeking out varied references across domains and styles forces you to decouple the design problem from the implementation

[Rubric] Related Works

Check Beall's List, which is a list of potentially predatory publishers and journals. You can also look for signs like: the journal charges high fees with little editorial oversight, it accepts papers very quickly with minimal peer review, or it's not indexed in major academic databases. When in doubt, do a quick search for the journal name alongside the word "predatory" or ask the Professor.

Your related works collection is meant to inspire your visual design, not your domain knowledge or analytical methods. A related work is relevant if the visualization technique could plausibly be applied to your dataset. This means the marks, channels, and idioms used in the source are compatible with the structure of your data: the attribute types, dataset types, and tasks involved. Topic overlap alone does not make something a valid related work.

For example, if your dataset has two quantitative attributes and a categorical grouping, a scatterplot with color encoding is relevant. A chord diagram showing relationships between nodes is not, unless your data has that relational structure or you plan to derive the relationships.

The listicle itself cannot be your source, you must cite the original publication or post. If you find the original, then yes, you can use it, as long as it meets the quality standards described in Part 0. Be aware that tracking down originals can be time-consuming; it may be more efficient to search for high-quality sources directly.
