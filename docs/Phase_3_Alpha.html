Overview

For the Alpha Release, your goals are to explore different encoding and design choices by coding and sketching a bunch of design artifacts, to then evaluate those choices using the effectiveness and expressiveness principles from class.

Course Themes

Throughout this course we have explored some themes around different design choices: Color (Ch. 10), Transformation (Ch. 2–3), Animation/Interaction (Ch. 11–12), Marks + Channels (Ch. 5, 7, 8). These themes should guide your design exploration and evaluation.

For the Alpha release, your group must engage deeply with at least two or three themes of your choice. “Deeply” means exploring multiple options, comparing and contrasting them, and evaluating trade-offs using course principles, not just mentioning a theme in passing. To do this, you’ll generate many design artifacts.

There are some example theme explorations at the end of this doc.

TipWhat is a design artifact?

A design artifact is any tangible output of your design process: something you created to explore, communicate, or evaluate a design idea. This includes hand-drawn sketches, digital mockups, wireframes, custom color palettes, annotated pseudocode, process diagrams, storyboards, user stories, digital mockups. The key distinction is that an artifact is something you made to think through a design choice, not something merely described in words. A napkin sketch comparing two layout options is a design artifact; a paragraph saying “we considered two layouts” is not.

There is no “correct” number of artifacts: a healthy dose of curiosity + continuous evaluation will get your team to the right number. The expectation for this phase is that you produce too many artifacts, and then prune to the most promising set based on your evaluation, your peers’ evaluations, and professor feedback.

Important

Minimum requirement: At least two (2) artifacts must be implemented in software. The rest can be sketches, mockups, or other non-code artifacts. More coded artifacts is always better to get a sense of feasibility.

Deliverables

Deliverable 1: Slide Deck

Your slides should include:

  • Labeled figures and screenshots of all design artifacts generated (coded and sketched), not just the ones presented in your video
  • Informative image captions on every figure i.e. not just “Figure 1” but what it shows and why it matters. Check out this resource for how to write great captions.
  • Key design decisions or trade-offs
Note

Your slides and video will be shared with your peer review group, so they should be clear enough for someone unfamiliar with your project to follow, and well-captioned visuals make their job easier.

Deliverable 2: Recorded Video Demo (8–9 minutes)

Think of this as showing your work to a sponsor who might fund your project. Not a formal pitch, just what are you building, why, and what have you explored so far?

Your video should include:

  1. Domain + Goal (∼30 sec) — What is your project about? What should a user be able to do with your visualization?
  2. Data Walk-Through (∼30 sec) — Open your data, show its structure. How many items? What attributes? Any preprocessing or joining you had to do?
  3. Code Walk-Through (2–3 min) — Walk through your code. Explain and show how it works, not just the output.
  4. Most Promising Designs (1–2 min) — Present and discuss one or two designs your group is most excited about. Coded or polished sketches.
  5. Artifact Evaluation (1–2 min) — For each artifact you present in step 4: Which tasks does it support? What did you explore to come to this decision? What trade-offs did you make?
  6. Next Steps (∼30 sec) — What improvements or new directions are you planning?

Production requirements:

  • Narrate with voice + captions, or use text annotations (call-outs, pop-ups). Closed-captions are required if you use voice, they can be auto-generated.
  • If using your mouse as a pointer, make sure the cursor is clearly visible
Important

Once a video assignment is submitted, Yuja doesn’t let students edit, replace, or delete the video file from their Media Collection, so make sure the final version is ready before anyone hits submit!

Peer Review Process

How It Works

  1. Assignment: Each group will be assigned one other group’s video and slide deck to review.
  2. Asynchronous Review: Watch the video, study the slides, and complete the peer evaluation rubric as a team. Every member of your group should watch the video.
  3. Live Review Presentation (10–15 min): Your group will present the other group’s project to the class. Walk the audience through what they built, what design choices they explored, and your evaluation of their work using the rubric.
Tip

Why this format? If five people conferred and worked to understand your project and still can’t explain it clearly, that’s important feedback. The clarity of your deliverables directly affects how well your reviewers can represent your work.

Your Review Presentation Will Cover

  • Project Overview: Summarize the domain, data, and visualization goals in your own words.
  • Design Artifacts: Walk through their key artifacts. Which ones are most effective and why? Which artifacts clash with principles we learned in class, and how could they be improved?
  • Rubric Evaluation: What did you like about the project? What do you wish the project did but doesn’t? What out of the box thinking could help the team innovate?
  • Recommendations: What would you suggest they prioritize for the Beta Release?

Example Theme Explorations

TipColor

Here are some examples for what you could do to explore Color

Experiment by comparing and contrasting multiple elements related to color, for example:

  • (Which color scale to use??
  • how many color bins to use?
  • can we use hot vs fire?
  • what is a diverging scale for?
  • how to use color with choropleths?)

You may want to try out multiple tools/packages for encoding with color in visualization, and ensure you can use the output in your project

  • Colorgorical
  • ColorBrewer
  • VizPalette
  • ProPlot Python library
  • ColorMoves

To evaluate the Color theme, consider: For your project’s users+tasks+data, what are the perceptual ramifications of using different types of colormaps? What is the effect of changing the color binning? How many attributes can you show at once with each color scheme? What contrast is needed for colors in your vis? Are there any cultural constructs around your selected colors?

Notes for the Color Theme:

  • Make sure you compare and contrast different options, rather than trying 1 and saying “looks good.”
  • If you choose to use a color tool, ensure you understand how to use the output of the tool in your project
TipTransformation

Here are some examples for what you could do to explore Transformation

  • Explore the effect of different abstractions: Abstractions
  • Transform Tabular data → network: Graphia
  • Explore different bar chart groupings: Basic grouped barplot in d3.js
  • Explore different clustering/hierarchies: Tree, Cluster / D3 | Observable, D3 Hierarchies

To evaluate the Transformation theme, investigate different ways to transform your data, and/or to derive new data, and answer some relevant theme specific questions. What new data could you derive to improve the match between data semantics and the task? Why? Do you need to normalize your data? Do you need to standardize your data? Why or why not? Can you try out different semantics: for example, if your initial dataset type is tabular, can you transform it to another dataset type? Which one? What advantages might this hold? Disadvantages? How might you need to aggregate attributes hierarchically or derive groupings to improve the effectiveness of your visualizations?

Notes for the Transformation Theme:

  • You may want to check if you need to get any additional supporting data to support your transformation. For example, latitude longitude of each country, country border polygons, GDP, county/state-level census data, etc.
TipAnimation or Interaction

Here are some examples for what you could do to explore Animation/ Interaction

  • Animating cartograms: A smooth transition between choropleth and cartogram
  • Animating transitions: How to Animate Transitions Between Multiple Charts | FlowingData
  • Scrollytelling: How to implement scrollytelling with six different libraries
  • Field animation: fieldanimation · PyPI
  • Fisheye distortion for tree views: Fisheye Distortion

To evaluate the Animation/Interaction theme, consider: what should the user be able to input and how will they input it? What should the user be able to customize, and via what widget (slider, direct input, radio button, etc) ? How many views will you have, and how will they be coordinated? How can you use animation to increase/decrease the effectiveness? What elements should be in an initial overview vs. a detail on-demand view? How might the user navigate within your system (zoom, pan, etc)? Would scrollytelling work for your case?

Notes for the Animation/Interaction Theme

  • Artifacts for this theme should be documented in the form of storyboards, wireframes, and/or user stories
TipMarks + Channels

Here are some examples for exploring Marks + Channels

  • Go to the zoo (the visualization zoo): A Tour through the Visualization Zoo - ACM Queue
  • Learn some new vocab (visual vocab): Visual Vocabulary
  • Choose a few from the Python graph gallery: Python Graph Gallery
  • Choose a few from the D3 graph library: The D3 Graph Gallery

To evaluate the Marks+Channels theme, after experimenting with different combinations of marks and channels relevant to the tasks you have identified, you could consider the scalability of these (i.e. what would happen if you try to break it with too much data). What visual channels can you use? For each visual channel you list, describe the data and attribute type it encodes. What marks can you use? How many attributes can you show at once with each combination of mark(s) + channel(s)? Which channels interfere, and how? Can you combine marks into glyphs?

Notes for the Marks+Channels Theme:

  • Use the concepts from Chapter 5 and Chapter 7 to describe the effects of each different idiom you create. Are you using best practices? Why or why not?
  • Make sure you compare and contrast different options, rather than trying only 1 and saying “looks good.”
